Prototype problem statement

Orchestration is easy to claim, hard to prove

In August 2026, Hippocratic AI moved from selling individual voice agents to selling Agentic Orchestrators teams of agents coordinated to move outcomes like readmission rates, HEDIS measures, and Star Ratings across a whole population. That's a much bigger claim than "the agent completed the call," and it raises a data problem underneath the product story: how do you actually measure whether the coordination layer is earning its keep, and how do you trust the patient data it's making decisions from in the first place?

This prototype tackles the first half of that question directly: a synthetic simulation that compares a naive, random agent-assignment policy against a simplified orchestrated one, on the same population, so the lift is measurable rather than assumed.

Research narrowed the target

No real patient data? Simulated it.

No PHI access, no company data room, so the constraint became the design.

  1. 01 Built a seeded synthetic-cohort generator instead of waiting for real records.
  2. 02 Modeled risk tiers, channel preference, and prior-readmission history from realistic distributions.
  3. 03 Kept every synthetic assumption visible in an on-page methodology section, not buried in code.

How it works

From a synthetic cohort to a measurable lift

Three steps, run entirely in the browser above.

1

Generate the cohort

A seeded random generator creates synthetic patients with a risk score, comorbidity count, channel preference, and readmission history.

2

Run two policies

Every patient is routed twice: once to a random agent, once to the agent matched to their risk profile. Same person, same noise, two strategies.

3

Compare the outcomes

Readmission risk, engagement resolution, and time-to-engagement are aggregated and charted, naive against orchestrated, by tier, channel, and agent.
